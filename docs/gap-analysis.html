Step 4 · Reading & gap analysis

Literature review & research gap

Zero-trust access control + a closed detect → alert → auto-mitigate response loop for healthcare data, against an outsider attacker. 26 sources read; the 22 that carry weight are listed with what each one actually establishes.

Read this part first. The literature is strong on detection, thin on closing the loop, and in healthcare specifically, zero-trust work is mostly frameworks with no working artifact. That combination is our opening — and one 2026 systematic review says so explicitly: detection models hit strong benchmarks, but "the operationalization of automated response within clinical settings remains nascent."

The gap

Across all 26 sources, the three capabilities we care about live in three separate literatures and have never been joined:

GAP 1 — Zero trust stops at the whiteboardHealthcare zero-trust papers (1–4) are frameworks, transition plans, or cost analyses. Not one implements per-request authorization for a clinical record store and shows it working.
GAP 2 — Tamper evidence is heavy, and aimed at the log, not the recordBlockchain work (5–13) protects access trails and sharing, at ledger cost. Lightweight per-record hash verification (7) exists but is invoked as a separate audit step, never inline on the write path with a response attached.
GAP 3 — Detection never closes the loopThe detection literature (14–22) is saturated, and the strongest signal in it is USENIX Security 2023 (16): anomaly detection on EHR access is evadable. The response literature (23–25) is reviews and vendor guidance. A 2026 PRISMA review states the gap in plain words: detection is measured, response is not implemented.
GAP 4 — Nobody measures the loop's costMTTD/MTTR are discussed qualitatively (23–25). No healthcare paper reports the latency of detect → alert → mitigate end-to-end per event — which is the only figure that decides whether automated response is clinically admissible.
GAP 5 — The closest match is not a paperSource 26 does most of what we propose, but as a thesis, without a zero-trust layer, without tamper evidence, and without latency numbers.

Our contribution, in one sentence

We present and empirically evaluate a four-layer architecture that unifies (i) zero-trust, role-and-request-gated access to patient records, (ii) lightweight per-record hash-chain tamper evidence, (iii) ML-plus-rule anomaly detection, and (iv) a closed, automatically executed detect → alert → mitigate loop with rollback — reporting both detection quality and the measured end-to-end latency of mitigation, under an outsider-attacker threat model.

Three claims we intend to defend, and one we explicitly will not make:

  • We claim integration: the literature has the parts, nobody has assembled them for healthcare with the response step wired up.
  • We claim measurable mitigation latency at microsecond scale for the integrity and response path — turning MTTR from a slogan into a number.
  • We claim that rule-based integrity evidence catches what the classifier misses, which is the honest lesson from our own r2l/u2r recall.
  • We do not claim a better detector. Our RandomForest on a stock NSL-KDD split lands mid-pack, and the papers reporting 99.9% are reporting a balanced-benchmark artifact. Claiming a detection accuracy win would be both false and unnecessary.
